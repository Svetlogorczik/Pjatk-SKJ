⏱️Network performance — delay, loss and throughput

The four components of packet delay, the caravan analogy, traffic intensity La/R, traceroute, packet loss and the bottleneck link — with formulas and step-by-step calculators.

In short#

  • Packet delay at one node is the sum of four components: dnodal=dproc+dqueue+dtrans+dpropd_{nodal} = d_{proc} + d_{queue} + d_{trans} + d_{prop}dnodal​=dproc​+dqueue​+dtrans​+dprop​.
  • dtrans=L/Rd_{trans} = L/Rdtrans​=L/R depends on packet size and link rate; dprop=d/sd_{prop} = d/sdprop​=d/s depends on link length — two completely different things!
  • Traffic intensity La/RLa/RLa/R: near 0 — small queues; near 1 — queues grow very fast; above 1 — "infinite" delay.
  • traceroute measures the delay to consecutive routers on the path.
  • End-to-end throughput = the rate of the slowest link on the path — the bottleneck.

Where do delay and loss come from?#

Packets queue in router buffers. This happens when the arrival rate to a link temporarily exceeds the output link capacity. Then:

  • one packet is being transmitted (transmission delay),
  • others wait in the buffer (queueing delay),
  • if there are no free buffers, an arriving packet is dropped (loss).

⚠️Warningdtransd_{trans}dtrans​ and dpropd_{prop}dprop​ are very different (a sentence from the lecture)
  • dtransd_{trans}dtrans​ does not depend on distance — only on how many bits must be pushed out and how fast the link accepts them.
  • dpropd_{prop}dprop​ does not depend on packet size — only on how far away the other end of the link is.
✍️Added by the site author — not in the lectureHow to remember it?

Imagine pouring water into a pipe: transmission is the time to pour the whole bucket (depends on bucket size and funnel width), propagation is the time for the first drop to reach the end of the pipe (depends on pipe length).

The caravan analogy (from the lecture)#

A caravan of 10 cars (a 10-bit "packet") drives through toll booths ("routers") that are 100 km apart.

  • Cars "propagate" at 100 km/h.
  • A toll booth needs 12 s to service one car (the "bit transmission time").
  • Car ≈ bit, caravan ≈ packet.

Question: how long until the whole caravan is lined up before the 2nd toll booth?

  1. Time to "push" the whole caravan through the booth (transmission): 12 s⋅10=120 s=212\ \text{s} \cdot 10 = 120\ \text{s} = 212 s⋅10=120 s=2 min.
  2. Time for the last car to drive to the 2nd booth (propagation): 100 km/(100 km/h)=1100\ \text{km} / (100\ \text{km/h}) = 1100 km/(100 km/h)=1 h =60= 60=60 min.
  3. Total: 2+60=622 + 60 = 622+60=62 minutes.

The second version from the lecture: cars now drive 1000 km/h and a booth needs 1 minute per car. Will cars arrive at the 2nd booth before all cars are serviced at the 1st?

  1. The first car leaves the booth after 1 min.
  2. It drives 100 km at 1000 km/h: 100/1000100/1000100/1000 h =6= 6=6 min.
  3. It reaches the 2nd booth after 1+6=71 + 6 = 71+6=7 min.
  4. By then 7 cars have passed the 1st booth — 3 are still waiting there.

Yes! The first bits of a packet can reach the next router while the last bits are still in the previous one (when dtrans>dpropd_{trans} > d_{prop}dtrans​>dprop​).

Queueing delay and traffic intensity#

Notation from the lecture:

  • RRR — link bandwidth [bit/s],
  • LLL — packet length [bit],
  • aaa — average packet arrival rate [packets/s].

Traffic intensity:

I=L⋅aRI = \frac{L \cdot a}{R}I=RL⋅a​
Value of La/RLa/RLa/RWhat happens to the queue
≈0\approx 0≈0average queueing delay is small
→1\to 1→1average queueing delay is large (grows very fast)
>1> 1>1more "work" arrives than can be serviced — average delay is infinite
🧩ExampleCompute the traffic intensity ✍️ author's note

Link R=10R = 10R=10 Mb/s, 1500 B packets, on average 500 packets per second.

  1. L=1500⋅8=12 000L = 1500 \cdot 8 = 12\,000L=1500⋅8=12000 bit.
  2. L⋅a=12 000⋅500=6 000 000L \cdot a = 12\,000 \cdot 500 = 6\,000\,000L⋅a=12000⋅500=6000000 bit/s.
  3. I=6⋅106/107=0.6I = 6 \cdot 10^6 / 10^7 = 0.6I=6⋅106/107=0.6 — queues are noticeable but stable.
  4. At 800 packets/s: I=0.96I = 0.96I=0.96 — huge delays. At 900/s: I=1.08>1I = 1.08 > 1I=1.08>1 — the queue grows forever, packets will be lost.
💡TipLesson for the project ✍️ author's note

Never design a system "at the limit" (I≈1I \approx 1I≈1). A tiny increase in traffic then causes a huge increase in delay — this also applies to your proxy if it serves clients one by one in a single thread.

Real delays: traceroute#

traceroute measures the delay from the source to every router on the path to the destination. How it works (from the lecture):

  1. For every iii it sends 3 packets that will reach router iii on the path (field TTL = i).
  2. Router iii returns packets to the sender.
  3. The sender measures the time between transmission and reply — the RTT to router iii.

A fragment of the lecture's example (path gaia.cs.umass.edu → www.eurecom.fr):

  • Each line is one router with 3 delay measurements.
  • The jump from 22 ms to 104 ms between hop 7 and 8 is a trans-oceanic link (long dpropd_{prop}dprop​).
  • * * * — no response (probe lost or the router doesn't reply).
✍️Added by the site author — not in the lectureWhy do delays sometimes "decrease" further along the path? (a lecture question)

Because every measurement is a separate packet sent at a different moment. Router queues change from millisecond to millisecond, so a packet to a farther router may simply have hit empty queues. Also, routers often answer such probes "with low priority".

Packet loss#

  • The queue (buffer) in front of a link has finite capacity.
  • A packet arriving to a full queue is dropped (lost).
  • A lost packet may be retransmitted by the previous node, by the source end system — or not at all.
✍️Added by the site author — not in the lectureWho retransmits? It depends on the protocol
  • TCP notices the missing acknowledgement and retransmits the segment — that's why with Java's Socket you don't worry about lost data.
  • UDP does nothing — that's why with DatagramSocket you must call setSoTimeout(...) yourself and decide what to do when no answer comes. See C5.

Throughput#

Throughput is the rate (bits per unit of time) at which bits arrive from sender to receiver:

  • instantaneous — at a given moment,
  • average — over a longer period.

The lecture compares links to pipes carrying a "fluid" of bits. A server sends a file of FFF bits to a client; the server's link is RsR_sRs​, the client's link is RcR_cRc​.

SituationAverage end-to-end throughput
Rs<RcR_s < R_cRs​<Rc​RsR_sRs​
Rs>RcR_s > R_cRs​>Rc​RcR_cRc​

Bottleneck link — the link on the end-to-end path that constrains the end-to-end throughput. In general:

throughput=min⁡(R1,R2,…,RN)\text{throughput} = \min(R_1, R_2, \dots, R_N)throughput=min(R1​,R2​,…,RN​)

The lecture's network scenario: 10 connections (server–client) fairly share a backbone link RRR:

per-connection throughput=min⁡(Rc, Rs, R10)\text{per-connection throughput} = \min\left(R_c,\ R_s,\ \frac{R}{10}\right)per-connection throughput=min(Rc​, Rs​, 10R​)

In practice the bottleneck is usually RcR_cRc​ or RsR_sRs​ (the access links), not the backbone.

🧩ExampleHow long does a download take? ✍️ author's note

A 4 MB file, server Rs=2R_s = 2Rs​=2 Mb/s, client Rc=10R_c = 10Rc​=10 Mb/s, backbone idle.

  1. F=4⋅8=32F = 4 \cdot 8 = 32F=4⋅8=32 Mb.
  2. Bottleneck: min⁡(2,10)=2\min(2, 10) = 2min(2,10)=2 Mb/s.
  3. Time: 32/2=1632 / 2 = 1632/2=16 s.

Check yourself ✍️ author's note#

❓Check yourselfA 1000 B packet, a 1 Mb/s link 2000 km long, s = 2·10⁸ m/s. What are d_trans and d_prop?

dtrans=8000/106=8d_{trans} = 8000 / 10^6 = 8dtrans​=8000/106=8 ms. dprop=2⋅106/(2⋅108)=0.01d_{prop} = 2 \cdot 10^6 / (2 \cdot 10^8) = 0.01dprop​=2⋅106/(2⋅108)=0.01 s =10= 10=10 ms.

❓Check yourselfTraffic intensity is 1.2. What happens?

More data arrives than the link can send. The queue grows forever, and once the buffer is full, packets are lost.

❓Check yourselfClient 50 Mb/s, server 100 Mb/s, a shared 400 Mb/s link used by 10 connections. What is the throughput of one connection?

min⁡(50,100,400/10)=min⁡(50,100,40)=40\min(50, 100, 400/10) = \min(50, 100, 40) = 40min(50,100,400/10)=min(50,100,40)=40 Mb/s — the shared link is the bottleneck.

❓Check yourselfWhy does traceroute send packets with TTL = 1, 2, 3…?

So that the packet "dies" exactly at router iii — that router sends a reply back, which tells us its address and the RTT to it.

📝 Cheat sheet: Network performance — delay, loss and throughput

Formulas#

  • dnodal=dproc+dqueue+dtrans+dpropd_{nodal} = d_{proc} + d_{queue} + d_{trans} + d_{prop}dnodal​=dproc​+dqueue​+dtrans​+dprop​
  • dtrans=L/Rd_{trans} = L / Rdtrans​=L/R (L in bits!)
  • dprop=d/sd_{prop} = d / sdprop​=d/s, s≈2⋅108s \approx 2 \cdot 10^8s≈2⋅108 m/s
  • Traffic intensity: I=La/RI = La / RI=La/R (≈0 small, →1 large, >1 infinite)
  • Throughput =min⁡(Rs,Rc,R/n)= \min(R_s, R_c, R/n)=min(Rs​,Rc​,R/n); download time =F/throughput= F / \text{throughput}=F/throughput
  • Path of NNN links: N⋅dnodalN \cdot d_{nodal}N⋅dnodal​ (textbook)

"Compute the delay" step by step#

  1. Write down the data and convert units: B → bit (×8), Mb/s → bit/s (×10⁶), km → m (×1000).
  2. dtrans=L/Rd_{trans} = L/Rdtrans​=L/R.
  3. dprop=d/sd_{prop} = d/sdprop​=d/s.
  4. Add dprocd_{proc}dproc​ and dqueued_{queue}dqueue​ (if given; usually 0).
  5. Multiply by the number of links (store-and-forward).
  6. Result in ms (×1000).

Caravan#

  • 10 cars × 12 s = 120 s + 100 km / 100 km/h = 60 min → 62 min.
  • 1000 km/h, 1 min/car → first car at booth 2 after 7 min, 3 cars still waiting.

traceroute#

  • TTL = i → router i answers; 3 measurements per router; * * * = no answer.
  • Windows: tracert, Linux/macOS: traceroute.
